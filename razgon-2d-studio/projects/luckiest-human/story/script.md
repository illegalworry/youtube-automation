# What If You Were the Luckiest Human Alive?

Format: second person ("you"), same as the 100%-brain video. Beats marked with [ON SCREEN]. Lines that do not start with `[` or `#` are narration.

## HOOK (0:00)

[ON SCREEN: black screen, one coin spinning in the dark, it lands on a table, heads, bright flash]

Every coin lands your way. Every door opens. And you have exactly nine days before it breaks you.

[ON SCREEN: title card, number pill "9 DAYS"]

## THE TURN (0:12)

Here is the deal. Tonight, while you sleep, something changes. Not your body. Not your brain. Your luck. Every random thing in the universe that could go your way, will.

[ON SCREEN: stick figure in bed, a soft golden glow settles over him like a blanket]

And I'm going to follow you for nine days to see if that is a gift or a trap.

## DAY ONE (0:30)

[ON SCREEN: morning, alarm clock, the figure wakes, stretches]

You wake up one minute before your alarm. No reason. You just feel rested.

You open the fridge. One egg left. You crack it, and it has two yolks.

[ON SCREEN: close-up, double yolk in a pan, a tiny sparkle]

You step outside. The bus pulls up the exact second you reach the stop. Every light on the way is green. Not most of them. Every one.

[ON SCREEN: bus route montage, row of green lights ticking on]

You laugh. This is nice. This is very nice.

Then you reach into your pocket, and there is a crumpled lottery ticket you bought three days ago and forgot about.

[ON SCREEN: ticket in hand, numbers glowing]

You check the numbers. Then you check them again.

Here is the thing about that ticket. In the biggest American lottery, the odds of matching everything are about one in 292 million. For comparison, if you flipped a coin and needed heads twenty-eight times in a row, you would be looking at roughly the same odds.

[ON SCREEN: coin row, 28 coins, all heads, counter "1 in 268 million", lottery "1 in 292 million" beside it]

You just did that. Without trying.

By lunch you are rich. Not "nice dinner" rich. Quit your job rich. Buy a house rich.

[ON SCREEN: stack of money, moving truck, the figure grinning]

And you think: this is the best day of my life.

## DAY TWO AND THREE (2:10)

[ON SCREEN: calendar pages flip, "DAY 2", "DAY 3"]

Day two, you want to test it. Of course you do. So you walk into a casino.

[ON SCREEN: neon casino, slot machines, roulette wheel]

You put everything on red. The ball spins. It lands on red. You put everything on red again. Red again.

The dealer stops smiling. A man in a suit starts watching you from across the room.

[ON SCREEN: security camera angle, a man in a suit whispering into a radio]

You are not cheating. There is nothing to catch. Nobody can find a trick, because there is no trick. You are just lucky, and luck does not leave fingerprints.

Still, they ask you to leave.

On day three you try something else. You cross a busy road without looking.

[ON SCREEN: wide shot, cars flash by, horns, the figure walks calmly through]

A truck brakes an inch before you. A bird lands on the hood at the perfect moment and the driver turns his head. A cyclist swerves around you like you are not even there.

You don't feel fear. That is the strange part. You can't remember the last time you felt afraid.

## THE PEOPLE WHO ALMOST DID THIS (4:00)

[ON SCREEN: archive photo style cards, "REAL STORIES"]

And you are not the first person to look absurdly lucky. History has a few people who look a lot like you.

There was a man named Tsutomu Yamaguchi. In August 1945 he was in Hiroshima on a business trip when the atomic bomb fell. He survived. He went home to Nagasaki, and a few days later, the second bomb fell there.

[ON SCREEN: two city silhouettes, two dates, a figure walking between them]

He survived that too. Japan officially recognized him as a survivor of both. Is that luck? Or is that the worst luck imaginable, being there both times?

And that question is the trick of this whole video, so hold on to it.

There was Violet Jessop, a ship stewardess who was aboard the Olympic when it collided with a warship, then aboard the Titanic, then aboard the Britannic when it sank. She lived through all three.

[ON SCREEN: three ships in a row, a lifeboat, a small figure waving]

There was a park ranger named Roy Sullivan who was struck by lightning seven times and survived every one.

[ON SCREEN: tall stick figure, seven small lightning bolts stacked beside him]

Look at those stories again. Every one of them is half terrible luck, half wonderful luck. The bad part makes the good part possible. You have to stand in the storm to be the one who walks out of it.

But you? You don't get the storm. You only get the walking out.

And that, it turns out, is where the problems begin.

## WHAT LUCK ACTUALLY IS (5:50)

[ON SCREEN: a million stick figures flipping coins, most turning gray and vanishing round by round]

Before we go further, I need to be fair to the universe. Because luck, in real life, is a sneaky thing.

Imagine a million people each flip a coin, and everyone who gets tails sits down. After one round, half a million are standing. After ten rounds, about a thousand. After twenty rounds, roughly one person is still on their feet, and everyone is staring at them.

[ON SCREEN: one lone figure under a spotlight, crowd of gray figures pointing]

That person will give interviews. They'll write a book. They'll say it was their morning routine.

It wasn't. It was arithmetic. With enough people playing, somebody always wins, and the winner always thinks it was about them.

Statisticians call this survivorship bias. We only hear from the people who walked out. The ones who didn't aren't invited to speak.

[ON SCREEN: a bright stage, a long dark corridor behind it full of silent silhouettes]

But in your case, there is no corridor. Nobody falls behind you. The filter is gone. You are the one on the stage forever, and the arithmetic has been rewritten around you.

Which sounds like a dream, right up until the moment you try to enjoy it.

## DAY FOUR (6:20)

[ON SCREEN: "DAY 4", the figure in a huge empty mansion, cold light]

On day four you wake up in your new house and nothing in it feels like yours.

You didn't pick the furniture. A designer did, because you were too busy winning. You didn't earn the money, it just arrived. You didn't pick your friends in the new neighborhood. They arrived too, smiling, asking if you want to invest in something.

[ON SCREEN: line of people at the door with folders and pens]

You say yes to one of them. Just to see. The investment triples in a week.

You say yes to another one, a terrible one, a plan so bad that your old accountant would have fainted.

[ON SCREEN: crossed-out business plan, a tiny restaurant on a cliff]

It triples too.

And somewhere in the middle of all that winning, a very quiet thought walks into your head.

What did I do?

[ON SCREEN: the figure sits on the floor, close up, eyes half-lidded]

Nothing. You did nothing. You made a choice, and the universe quietly erased the risk from it.

## THE MATH OF A GOOD DAY (8:00)

[ON SCREEN: blueprint style diagram, a branching tree of outcomes, most branches fading out]

Here is something most people never think about. Every choice you've ever made has a tree behind it. Branches where it worked. Branches where it didn't.

When you pick a restaurant, you are not just picking dinner. You are picking a little bundle of possible evenings. Some great. Some average. A few terrible.

The terrible ones are the reason the great ones feel great.

Psychologists have a name for the idea that we judge things by comparison, not by absolute value. A cold glass of water feels like heaven only after a long walk in the sun. The same glass on a rainy Tuesday is just water.

[ON SCREEN: two glasses of water, one in a desert, one on a desk, same glass, different faces]

Your luck deletes the desert. You will never be thirsty again. And a person who is never thirsty has no idea what water is.

## DAY FIVE AND SIX (9:40)

[ON SCREEN: "DAY 5", a stadium full of people, scoreboard glowing]

On day five you go to a football match with a friend.

Your team is down by three with one minute left. Your friend is screaming. Strangers are hugging. A kid beside you is holding his scarf so tight his knuckles turn white.

[ON SCREEN: tense crowd, hands over faces]

And you are just sitting there. Calm.

Because you already know. You can't explain how, but you just know, deep in your stomach, that it's going to work out. It always does.

The ball goes in. The whole stadium explodes. Your friend jumps on your shoulders.

[ON SCREEN: crowd erupts, confetti, the figure standing still in the middle]

And you feel nothing.

You smile, because that is what faces do. But inside it is flat, like a song played one note at a time.

Day six, you try to find something that can still scare you. You climb a mountain without a rope. You swim in rough water. You drive too fast with the windows down.

[ON SCREEN: cliff edge, wind, the figure steps out; a hand-sized rock hits the ledge and rolls away harmlessly]

Nothing hurts you. Nothing even comes close. And slowly you realize why people climb mountains in the first place. It isn't to be safe. It is to feel the rope.

## THE FAMILY DINNER (10:50)

[ON SCREEN: a long table, Mom, Dad, Grandma, a big cake, candles]

That night your family throws you a dinner. Your mom cooked all day. Your dad tells the story of the time you fell off your bike, a story he has told a hundred times.

You listen. You really do. But when the story ends, you realize you have no story of your own to add.

Every story you have starts the same way. Something wonderful happened, and then something else wonderful happened.

[ON SCREEN: speech bubbles above the table, Dad's bubble full of crashes and bandages, yours a flat row of gold stars]

Think about it. The stories people tell at a table are almost never about perfect days. They're about the flat tire in the middle of nowhere, the wrong train, the lost wallet that turned into a three-hour adventure with a stranger.

The mess is the memory.

Grandma leans over and pats your hand. "You look tired, sweetheart."

"I'm not," you say. "That's the problem. I never am."

[ON SCREEN: Grandma tilting her head, a warm, knowing smile]

She doesn't say anything clever. She just squeezes your hand a little tighter, as if she knows something you are about to find out.

## THE FRIEND PROBLEM (11:20)

[ON SCREEN: "DAY 7", a small apartment, two mugs, an old friend at the table]

Day seven, an old friend visits. Someone from before the money.

She is going through a hard time. Her small business is failing. She asks you for nothing, but you can see it on her face.

So you help. Of course you do.

You put money in. You introduce her to the right people. You do everything right.

[ON SCREEN: documents, signatures, a sparkling "approved" stamp]

And everything works. Her shop recovers in weeks. She cries, she hugs you, she says thank you.

But three days later you overhear her talking to someone on the phone.

"Honestly? I don't think I did anything. It was all him. He just... makes things work out."

[ON SCREEN: the figure outside the door, a thought bubble that slowly empties]

She isn't being cruel. She is just being accurate.

And suddenly you understand something that no lottery ticket ever mentioned. If luck does everything for you, you don't get to be anyone's hero. You don't get to be anyone's anything. You are a weather system, not a person.

## THE ONE THING LUCK CAN'T TOUCH (13:10)

[ON SCREEN: "DAY 8", rain on a window, the figure alone]

Day eight is the worst one.

You sit in your kitchen and you try to find one thing that can go wrong. Just one. You want it so badly it makes you laugh at yourself.

You flip a coin and call tails. It's tails. You flip it again, and call heads. Heads.

[ON SCREEN: coin flips in slow motion, each landing exactly as called, the figure's face going blank]

You throw a glass at the wall. It bounces off a pillow that appeared out of nowhere.

[ON SCREEN: the glass in mid-air, a pillow slides into frame]

Then you remember something. A girl you used to see at the coffee shop. Before everything. She laughed at your terrible jokes. You never asked her name.

You find her. Or maybe luck finds her for you. It doesn't matter.

You sit across from her. You say something, and she smiles.

[ON SCREEN: two figures at a small table, warm light, a candle]

And for the first time in eight days, you are scared. Really scared.

Because here is the one question your luck can't answer for you.

Does she like me?

[ON SCREEN: the figure's hands shaking, a single bead of sweat]

Luck can bend a coin. It can bend traffic lights, stock prices, falling bricks, and a roulette ball. But a person is not a coin. A person is a mind, and a mind is not a random event. Whatever she feels, she feels it for her own reasons.

You've been searching all week for a risk you could still lose.

And she is it.

## DAY NINE (15:20)

[ON SCREEN: "DAY 9", the figure alone in the street at sunrise]

Day nine, you ask her to dinner. You do it badly. You say it too fast and your voice cracks.

She looks at you. She is quiet for what feels like an hour.

[ON SCREEN: slow push in, her face, half in shadow]

Then she says: "I can't. I'm sorry."

And you stand there. And you feel it. A hit in the chest. Sharp, heavy, real.

[ON SCREEN: the figure's shoulders drop, then slowly lift, a tiny smile]

It is the best you've felt in nine days.

It hurts, and the hurt tells you something wonderful: you are still in there. You are still a person who can lose.

You walk home slowly. You notice the cracked sidewalk. You notice the smell of bread from a bakery. You notice that the sky is a very specific kind of pink.

For nine days nothing could touch you. Now one small no has opened the whole world back up.

## THE TWIST (17:30)

[ON SCREEN: wide shot, the city at dawn, a thin gold thread of light between rooftops]

Now here is the part I promised.

Remember Tsutomu Yamaguchi? The man who was in both cities?

You asked, was that the luckiest man in the world, or the unluckiest?

The answer is: he was alive. And that is the answer for every one of us.

[ON SCREEN: chain of numbers, each smaller, narrowing to a single dot]

Think about the odds of you, just you, sitting here. A particular egg, a particular cell, out of millions. And your parents meeting, and their parents, all the way back through every war, every winter, every wrong turn that somehow missed.

Nobody can give you an exact number for that. Anyone who does is guessing. But it is a staggeringly small chance, and it landed on you.

You are already the luckiest person alive. Not because you win everything. Because you get to try.

[ON SCREEN: the figure stops at the edge of the water, sunrise, quiet]

## THE ENDING (19:00)

[ON SCREEN: the golden glow lifts off the figure and drifts away, like a coat taken off]

On the morning of day ten, the glow goes. You can feel it leave. The first thing that happens is that you miss your bus.

[ON SCREEN: bus pulling away, the figure running after it]

You run after it, you don't make it, you stand there out of breath, and you start to laugh.

You laugh so hard that a stranger asks if you're okay.

You say, "I'm great. I'm really great."

[ON SCREEN: the figure grinning, hands on knees, the stranger confused]

The lottery money is gone. The mansion is gone. Most of the friends are gone, which is fine. The girl from the coffee shop is still just a girl you asked once.

But you have a bruise on your knee from running. A cold coffee in your hand. An unread message from your old friend: "Lunch? I'll pay. You owe me."

[ON SCREEN: phone screen, message bubble, a small heart appears]

You write back: "Deal. But I'm picking the place, and I'm warning you, it might be terrible."

[ON SCREEN: the figure typing, then smiling at the screen]

It might be. The food could be cold. The waiter could forget your order. It might rain on the way and you could forget your umbrella.

And you can't wait.

Because for nine days you had every outcome handed to you, and you would trade all nine of them for one evening where the ending is still a secret.

And you realize that the luckiest humans alive are not the ones who never lose.

They are the ones who still get to find out.
