Hundreds of people in one city danced for days in 1518, and nobody could make them stop.

Strasbourg, July. A woman named Frau Troffea walks into the street and starts to dance. No music. No party. Just dancing.

Within a week, thirty-four people had joined her. Within a month, around four hundred.

They danced until their shoes filled with blood. Some collapsed. Some, reportedly, never got up.

Luckily, the city doctors had a diagnosis: hot blood. Unluckily, their cure was more dancing.

So the council opened the guildhalls, hired musicians, and paid professional dancers to keep everyone moving.

It got worse.

Historians still argue about why. Moldy rye? Probably not. That poison causes cramps, not choreography. The best guess is stress. Hunger, disease, and a saint everyone believed could curse sinners with dancing.

It spreads like a yawn in a quiet room. One person starts, and everyone nearby catches it.

So the council changed its mind. Music banned. Dancing banned. The dancers were sent to a shrine, in red shoes.

By September, it was over. And what finally worked was the opposite of the first cure: silence.
