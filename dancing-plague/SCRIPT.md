# The Dancing Plague of 1518. Script v1 (draft for approval)

## Facts and sources
- July 1518, Strasbourg (Holy Roman Empire): a woman, "Frau Troffea", began dancing in the street and kept going for days.
- About 34 people had joined within a week; roughly 400 within a month (per Strasbourg-area chronicles, via Waller).
- Participants danced to exhaustion; there are reports of collapse and deaths. The popular "15 deaths a day" figure is NOT supported by surviving records, so the script says "some, reportedly".
- Local physicians blamed "hot blood". The council's response was to encourage dancing: guildhalls opened, musicians and professional dancers hired, later a wooden stage in the grain market.
- The council then reversed course: music and dancing banned, dancers sent in carts to the shrine of St Vitus at Saverne, given red shoes marked with crosses. The episode faded by September.
- Causes: ergot (mouldy rye) poisoning is the old theory and is disputed (ergot gives cramps, gangrene and convulsions, not coordinated dancing for days). Leading modern view: mass psychogenic illness driven by famine, disease and belief in St Vitus's curse.
- Sources: John Waller, *A Time to Dance, a Time to Die* (2008) and "In a spin: the mysterious dancing epidemic of 1518", Endeavour 32(3), 2008; Strasbourg council minutes and chronicles of the period; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Dancing mania".
- Everyday analogy: a yawn spreading through a quiet room.

## Voice-over (~225 words, target 60-65 s at ~3.7 words/s)

Hundreds of people in one city danced for days in 1518, and nobody could make them stop.

Strasbourg, July. A woman named Frau Troffea walks into the street and starts to dance. No music. No party. Just dancing.

Within a week, thirty-four people had joined her. Within a month, around four hundred.

They danced until their shoes filled with blood. Some collapsed. Some, reportedly, never got up.

Luckily, the city doctors had a diagnosis: hot blood. Unluckily, their cure was more dancing.

So the council opened the guildhalls, hired musicians, and paid professional dancers to keep everyone moving.

It got worse.

Historians still argue about why. Moldy rye? Probably not. That poison causes cramps, not choreography. The best guess is stress. Hunger, disease, and a saint everyone believed could curse sinners with dancing.

It spreads like a yawn in a quiet room. One person starts, and everyone nearby catches it.

So the council changed its mind. Music banned. Dancing banned. The dancers were sent to a shrine, in red shoes.

By September, it was over. And what finally worked was the opposite of the first cure: silence.
