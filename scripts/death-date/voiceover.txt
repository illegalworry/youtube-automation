About 170,000 people die every day. What if every one of them, and every one of you, could see the date coming?

Not a guess. Not a horoscope. The exact day, glowing quietly above every head on the planet.

Here's how this works. One morning, everyone wakes up and above each person there's a date. Yours, your mom's, the stranger on the bus, the newborn in the hospital down the street. Nobody knows where it came from. Nobody can turn it off. And within a few days, the world realizes the dates are never wrong.

So let's follow it through. First the first hour, then the first year, then the generation that never knew a world without the numbers. And by the end, I'll tell you the one thing about this world that's scarier than the dates.

The first thing every person on Earth does is look at their own number. And here's the strange part: you can't see the date above your own head directly. You need a mirror, a camera, or another person to tell you.

So the very first moment of this new world is about trust. Somebody has to read your date to you. And you have to decide whether you want to hear it.

Most people do the math right away. Global life expectancy is about 73 years. If you're 25, you'd quietly hope to see a date somewhere around the 2070s. And for most people, that's roughly what they get. Relief. A weird, shaky relief.

But not for everyone. Somewhere in every city, every school, every office, there are people whose date is this year. This month. Some of them, tomorrow.

And now you hit the second part of the hook. You don't just see your own date. You see everyone's. Your parents. Your partner. Your kids. Within the first hour, millions of families learn that someone they love doesn't have long. And there's nothing they can do about it.

By the end of the first week, the world has run its tests. People with dates that day die that day. From illness, from accidents, from things nobody saw coming. Doctors try to save them and fail. And the scary conclusion sinks in: the date doesn't predict your death. It just knows it.

And that leads to the first big twist. If your date is decades away, then, at least in this world, nothing kills you before it. You could be reckless. You could jump out of a plane without a parachute. And you'd survive. Probably badly hurt. But alive.

This is where things get dangerous in a new way. Because the date protects your life. It doesn't protect your body. People who feel invincible take risks they can't actually afford, and hospitals fill up with people who are guaranteed to live, just maybe not to walk.

And hospitals change overnight. Triage, the system of deciding who gets treated first, suddenly has a new question. If two people come in, and one has a date tomorrow and one has a date in forty years, who gets the last bed? Some doctors argue: treat the one who will live, because you know the effort won't be wasted. Others argue: the one with one day left deserves comfort and dignity. Medicine has never had to answer that question this clearly.

Then the economy notices. And the first industry to collapse is life insurance. The whole business runs on not knowing when you'll die. Insurers bet on the averages. Now everyone knows their exact date. People with short dates rush to buy huge policies. People with long dates cancel theirs. The math breaks in a matter of days.

Banks are next. A thirty-year mortgage suddenly looks very different when the lender can see that you only have twelve years. Governments try to ban "date discrimination" almost immediately. But you can't really ban someone from seeing what's above your head.

Employers start doing the same thing, quietly. Why train someone for five years if they'll be gone in three? Why promote someone whose date is next spring? And now there's a new kind of inequality, one you can't hide, can't change, and can't work your way out of.

So, of course, people try to cover them. Hats. Hoods. Umbrellas. Then a whole fashion industry built around hiding the one thing about you that's always visible. In some countries, covering your date becomes a basic right. In others, it's illegal, because police and governments want to see it.

And then there's crime. The dates change justice in ways nobody's ready for. Picture a courtroom. The defendant is guilty, and his date is two years away. What does a twenty-year sentence even mean now? The judge can see that it's really a two-year sentence. Is that fair to the victim? And what about someone with sixty years left getting the same twenty years? Same crime, same sentence, completely different punishment.

Detectives get a strange new tool. In our world, when someone dies suddenly, the first question is often "was this an accident?" In this world, a date can't tell you how someone dies, only when. But it can tell you who was never in danger. If a man claims he was attacked and almost killed, and his date is forty years away, then he was never going to die that night, whatever happened.

And here's the darkest question of all. If you see that someone's date is today, and you're the one who hurts them, did you cause their death, or were you just part of a death that was always going to happen? Philosophers in this world would argue about it forever. Courts would have to decide in an afternoon. Most of them, I think, would land on the same answer: the date shows the day, not the hand. You're still responsible for what you do. Fate is not a defense.

Prisons would have to deal with something new too: hundreds of thousands of inmates, all able to see exactly how much of their remaining life the sentence takes. Some countries would start releasing people a few weeks before their date, so they can die at home. Others would refuse. And every one of those decisions would make the news.

But let's step away from the systems and come back to people. Because the biggest change isn't in banks or hospitals. It's in how you spend your days.

Think about someone with two years left. In our world, they might not find out until it's almost over, if they find out at all. In this world, they know right away. And a lot of them do something remarkable. They stop waiting.

They call the people they've been avoiding. They say the thing they never said. They leave the job they hated. Two years can be a lifetime if you know it's two years.

Now think about the opposite. Someone with sixty years left. What's the rush? There's always next year. Next decade. And some psychologists in this world would worry that long dates make people lazy. Not because they're bad people, but because urgency is a big part of what pushes us to act.

So here's an odd paradox. In this world, the people with the least time might live the most deliberately. And the people with the most time might waste the most of it.

And then there's love. Imagine you meet the person of your dreams, and above their head is a date ten years from now, and above yours, fifty. Do you still marry them? Many people would say yes, absolutely. Ten years with the right person beats fifty with the wrong one. But for the first time in history, you'd be making that choice with your eyes fully open. Every anniversary would be a countdown you can both see.

And yes, someone would build a dating app with a "minimum years left" filter. It would be one of the most downloaded apps on Earth, and one of the most hated.

Now let's go to the hardest part. Babies are born with their dates already showing. The very first thing a parent sees, after their child's face, is the day that child will die.

For most families, the date is far away. Eighty, ninety years. That's a gift. A promise, almost. But for some families, the date is short. Five years. One year. Days.

How do you raise a child you know will be gone at six? Some parents would pull away, trying to protect their own hearts. Many more, I think, would do the opposite. They'd pour everything into those six years. Every birthday would be huge. Every ordinary Tuesday would matter.

And then there's the question that would divide the world: do you tell the child? Kids can't see their own date without a mirror. But they'd learn eventually. Would you want to grow up knowing? Or would you want your parents to protect you from it as long as they could?

And some families would face something that breaks the natural order completely. A grandparent with twenty years left, holding a grandchild with ten. In our world, we expect to bury our parents, not our children. In this world, some parents would know from the very first day that they'll have to. Support groups would form in every town. People who'd learned to live alongside a date they couldn't change, helping new parents through the first terrible weeks. And slowly, painfully, they'd teach each other the same lesson: a short life isn't a smaller life.

Now zoom out to the biggest question of all. When the dates first appear, places of worship around the world fill up like never before. Because the dates feel like proof of something. Proof that somewhere, somehow, it's all been written down.

Some people would see the dates as the ultimate sign of a plan. A higher power that knows every life from beginning to end. Others would be terrified by exactly the same thing. If the day you die is already fixed, how much of the rest is fixed too? Your job? Your choices? The person you'll fall in love with?

Scientists would throw everything at it. Cameras, sensors, brain scans. They'd try to measure the light, find where it comes from, block it, change it. And probably fail. Because nothing in physics as we know it explains a glowing number that knows the future.

And yet, I think most people would end up somewhere in the middle. They'd stop asking where the dates came from and start asking a simpler question. Okay, so I know when. Now what do I want my life to mean? And honestly, that might be the most important question the dates ever force anyone to ask. The number gives you the length of the story. It says nothing about what the story is about.

Now fast-forward. Fifty years later, nobody alive remembers the world before the dates. For them, this isn't a disaster. It's just normal. And normal changes everything.

Schools teach kids how to plan a life of a known length. Not a one-size-fits-all plan: a plan built around your number. A kid with twenty years might skip university and travel. A kid with ninety might study for a decade.

Funerals change too. In this future world, many people throw parties before their date, not after. They gather everyone they love, say goodbye properly, and spend their last day exactly how they want. Death stops being an ambush and becomes something closer to a planned departure.

And this future generation might look back at us, at you, right now, and find our world terrifying. A world where anyone could die at any time, without warning, without a goodbye. To them, our world would sound like a horror movie. We live with that uncertainty every single day, and we barely think about it.

So, at the start I promised you the one thing in this world that's scarier than the dates themselves. Here it is.

It's that nothing you do matters to the number. You can eat well, exercise, never take a risk, and the date won't move a single day. You can be reckless, and it won't move either. In our world, your choices shape how long you live. In this world, they don't. The number takes that power away from you.

Think about how much of your life right now is built around staying alive a little longer. Seatbelts. Checkups. Vegetables you don't really like. In this world, people with long dates would slowly stop bothering. And people with short dates would realize that no amount of effort buys them one extra sunrise. That's a kind of helplessness humans have never had to feel this directly. Not the fear of death, but the knowledge that you have zero say in it.

But here's what the dates can't take away: what you do with the time. The number decides how much. You still decide what's in it. And when you think about it, that's exactly our situation right now, except we don't get to see the number.
